# What standard of care actually is for this child, and what can be done

> **Re-read against the clinical record (27 Sep 2026).** This page was written as if
> surveillance were prophylactic. It is not: the proband's presenting event was
> **rhabdomyosarcoma** — the tumour type the genotype predicts — so what is owed is
> **survivor surveillance** for relapse and second primaries. The expected-benefit
> argument below gets *stronger*, because this child has demonstrated the risk rather
> than merely carrying it. But the protocol content shifts, and the LFS-derived
> figures quoted here are for surveillance of at-risk carriers, not for survivors of
> an index embryonal tumour. **Treat the direction as supported and the numbers as
> the wrong reference class.** See `PHENOTYPE-GROUNDING.md`.


Written 10 Sep 2026, after sweeping the MVA literature and the surveillance and
treatment-toxicity literature for comparable paediatric predisposition syndromes.

**This is analysis for a research hackathon, not clinical advice.** Every decision
here belongs to the child's own clinical team, who know things this document cannot.

---

## 1. There is no disease-modifying treatment for MVA. There never has been.

No registered clinical trial in mosaic variegated aneuploidy exists anywhere — an
exhaustive scan of ClinicalTrials.gov, ChiCTR, UMIN/JRCT, EudraCT/CTIS/ISRCTN and WHO
ICTRP returns zero. The entire therapeutic record is case reports, compiled in
`MVA-THERAPEUTIC-EVIDENCE-BASE.md`. Nothing in it targets the underlying checkpoint
defect, because a missing spindle-assembly checkpoint cannot be restored with a drug.

Standard of care today is therefore exactly what the hackathon page says it is:
**managing symptoms and watching for cancer.** That is not a gap in the literature —
it is the literature.

## 2. But "watching for cancer" is not a passive activity, and it is where lives are saved

This is the most important thing found in the whole sweep, and it does not come from
MVA research. It comes from the syndromes MVA most resembles: childhood-onset,
multi-tumour, high-penetrance predisposition.

**Li-Fraumeni syndrome.** A prospective observational study with 11 years of follow-up
found **5-year overall survival of 88.9% in the surveillance group versus 59.6% in the
non-surveillance group** [1]. The AACR protocol is abdominal and pelvic ultrasound
**every 3–4 months from birth to 18**, annual brain MRI from birth, and annual
whole-body MRI [1].

**Constitutional mismatch repair deficiency.** Surveillance produced a significant
survival benefit [2]. Protocol: brain MRI every 6 months, whole-body MRI annually from
age 6, abdominal ultrasound and full blood count every 6 months [2].

**Rhabdoid tumour predisposition.** No universally accepted protocol, but proposals of
brain MRI and abdominal ultrasound **every 3 months to age 5** [3].

The pattern is consistent: in syndromes where tumours arrive early and often,
structured surveillance changes survival substantially. There is no reason from first
principles to think MVA is different, and this child's genotype — biallelic *BUB1B* —
is in the high-risk group by the Yost stratification.

**MVA's own published suggestions already point the same way**, though informally and
only in case reports: "regular abdominal ultrasound surveillance, to particularly look
for Wilms tumor" [4], and "abdominal ultrasound, fundus examination, and hematological
checkup are advised **every 3 months**" [5].

**CORRECTION, 6 Oct 2026 — a consensus guideline now exists, and this document
previously said it did not.** The AACR Childhood Cancer Predisposition Workshop
(second workshop, July 2023) published updated surveillance recommendations for
genomic-instability disorders **explicitly including mosaic variegated aneuploidy**:
Nakano et al., *Clin Cancer Res* 30:5009–5020 (2024), doi:10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-24-1098.
This is expert multidisciplinary consensus, not a case report, and it supersedes the
"informal, case-report only" framing above. It strengthens rather than weakens this
document's conclusion: the one intervention we say should actually be acted on turns out
to carry guideline-level backing that we had failed to find.

It also corroborates §3 directly, at guideline level: these disorders *"present with
exquisite sensitivity to anticancer treatments such as ionizing radiation and
chemotherapy, which may further increase the risk of second cancers."* Note the
direction — the guideline-level concern about cytotoxic therapy in this group is
**excess toxicity**, not the reduced efficacy this workstream previously argued from
cell-line data (retracted; see `CORRECTIONS-2026-10-06.md` C1).

## 3. The most dangerous moment is not now — it is the day treatment starts

This is the part that surprised me most, and it may be the single most consequential
thing in this document.

Across DNA-repair and chromosomal-instability syndromes, **excess treatment toxicity is
a defining feature**, and it is severe:

> "managing such a patient must balance offering potentially curative therapy with the
> risk of **severe and even life-threatening toxicity**… not only an increased risk and
> severity of expected toxicities… but also unexpected toxicities such as
> cardiotoxicity and hepatotoxicity as well as deterioration of pre-existing
> comorbidities, especially of renal and pulmonary function" [6]

Consensus guidance for related syndromes now specifies **pre-emptive dose reduction**:
a 50% upfront reduction of alkylators, and high-dose methotrexate reduced to two-thirds,
for patients with ataxia-telangiectasia or Nijmegen breakage syndrome [7]. In Bloom
syndrome, nine children treated for cancer in France produced five remissions but with
"significant toxicities and high mortality" [8]. And where the syndrome was *not* known
in advance, the result has been fatal — a patient given cisplatin/gemcitabine died of
myelotoxicity, with Fanconi anaemia diagnosed only post mortem [9].

For MVA specifically, one clinical team has already acted on this logic: in a *CDC20*
MVA patient with endometrial carcinoma, they **"decided not to administer adjuvant
chemotherapy using taxanes"** because of the diagnosis [10]. Taxanes are spindle
poisons — the last thing to give someone whose spindle checkpoint is already broken.

**So the highest-value intervention for this child may be entirely non-pharmacological:
that whichever oncology team eventually treats them knows the diagnosis before the
first dose, and has a modification plan agreed in advance.** Excess toxicity in these
syndromes is often discovered retrospectively. It does not have to be.

## 4. What is concretely actionable

Ordered by what the evidence actually supports, not by what is novel:

1. **A formal, written surveillance protocol**, adapted from the LFS/CMMRD templates to
   MVA's tumour spectrum (Wilms, rhabdomyosarcoma, leukaemia). The MVA case reports
   already converge on ~3-monthly abdominal ultrasound; LFS uses 3–4 monthly abdominal
   and pelvic ultrasound to 18. Haematological monitoring matters independently here —
   the one MVA1 patient who reached HSCT did so via myelodysplasia with monosomy 7 [11].
2. **A pre-agreed treatment-modification plan**, written down now, while nobody is in
   crisis. At minimum it should record: avoid spindle poisons (taxanes, vinca alkaloids)
   on the reasoning above and the *CDC20* precedent [10]; expect and plan for excess
   toxicity from alkylators and radiation; consider upfront dose reduction on the AT/NBS
   model [7].
3. **Do not use growth hormone.** It is documented **ineffective** in MVA — "growth
   hormone treatment is ineffective in such patients" — and separately cautioned on
   cancer-risk grounds [12]. One team reflected: "Had this been known, growth hormone
   might have not been prescribed" [13].
4. **Symptomatic management**, which is most of daily life: seizures (frequently
   drug-resistant in MVA1 [5]), feeding, renal function, vision.
5. **Genetic counselling and family testing** — heterozygous carriers may not be
   entirely unaffected [14].

## 5. Where drug repurposing honestly fits

Lower than I would like, and the submission should say so.

The IL-1 leads (anakinra, canakinumab) remain a defensible hypothesis with a real
mechanistic argument, and they are the safest-looking class the funnel found. But the
evidence for them in MVA is zero, the two human datasets disagree, and the MAD1L1
authors argue for **exploiting** the aneuploidy-associated immune response rather than
blunting it [15].

**For this child, expected benefit from surveillance done well is almost certainly
greater than expected benefit from any repurposed drug we can currently propose.** A
Track 2 submission that says that, while still doing the repurposing work rigorously,
is more honest and more useful than one that implies a drug is around the corner.

That is not an argument against the funnel. Two of its outputs are already clinically
relevant in a way that does not depend on the IL-1 thesis at all: the safety gate's
refusal of mitotic agents is corroborated by real practice [10], and the compiled
therapeutic record shows which things have already been tried and failed.
